Who it is for

Leaders, managers, L&D and HR professionals, programme and capability managers, and business owners who have been asked to fix a performance problem and want to be sure they are fixing the right one. It applies in Defence and the wider public sector, in regulated and operational environments, and in commercial organisations of any size.

How to use it

Read chapters 1 to 4 for the thinking behind the method. Chapters 5 to 13 follow the order in which a diagnosis is usually carried out. The worksheets in chapter 14 can be printed and used in a workshop or completed on screen with a PDF editor. You do not need to complete every worksheet: use the smallest set that gives you enough evidence for a defensible decision.

A note on scope

This playbook sets out principles and practical tools. It is not a substitute for a facilitated review of your organisation, and it does not establish compliance with any standard or regulation. Where a sector has mandatory requirements (for example JSP 822 in Defence, or a professional regulator’s standards), those requirements take precedence.

01What organisational capability means

Capability is an organisation’s ability to achieve a defined outcome, to a required standard, repeatedly and under realistic conditions.

That definition matters because it is broader than the skills of the people involved. A team of highly competent individuals can still lack capability if the process they work within is unclear, the system they rely on is unreliable, accountability is split between three managers, or nobody has defined what “good” looks like.

Capability, competence and training are different things

TermWhat it describesTypical question
CapabilityWhat the organisation can reliably achieve, combining people, processes, systems, structure, leadership and resources.Can we deliver this outcome to the standard required, every time it is needed?
CompetenceAn individual’s demonstrated ability to perform a role or task to a defined standard.Can this person do the task, to standard, in the real working context?
TrainingOne of several ways of developing knowledge and skill.Would building knowledge or skill close the gap we have found?

Training contributes to competence. Competence contributes to capability. Neither relationship is automatic. When performance falls short, the useful question is not “what training do we need?” but “which part of the capability is missing or not working?”

Capability is a system

Capability comes from several elements working together. If any one is missing, the others cannot compensate for long:

  • People: enough of the right people, with the knowledge, skills and behaviours the work requires.
  • Process: a clear, workable way of doing the work, with defined standards.
  • Systems and tools: technology, equipment and information that support the task rather than obstruct it.
  • Structure and roles: clear responsibilities, authority and hand-offs.
  • Leadership and management: direction, priorities, feedback and the conditions for people to perform.
  • Governance and assurance: the means of knowing whether the standard is being met, and acting when it is not.
  • Resources and environment: time, budget, workload and working conditions that make the standard achievable.
If any part of the capability system is missing, training the people inside it will rarely close the gap on its own.

02Why training is not always the answer

Training is easy to commission, easy to count and easy to defend. That is exactly why it is so often chosen before anyone has established what the problem is.

A performance problem is usually reported as a request: “we need a course on …”, “the team needs refresher training”, “can you build some e-learning?”. The request describes a solution. It does not describe the problem, the cause or the outcome the organisation needs.

When training is the right answer

Training is likely to help when all of the following are true:

  • people do not know how to do the task, or cannot do it to standard, even when conditions are right;
  • they would perform correctly if their life depended on it only after being shown how;
  • the task is performed often enough, or is critical enough, to justify developing the skill rather than providing a job aid;
  • the work environment will allow them to apply what they learn.

When it is unlikely to be enough

Training rarely resolves problems caused by:

Process

Unclear, conflicting or unworkable procedures; standards that are not defined or not shared.

Systems and information

Tools that are slow, unreliable or badly designed; information that is missing, late or hard to find.

Roles and structure

Unclear accountability, overlapping responsibilities, hand-offs nobody owns.

Leadership and incentives

Priorities that conflict with the desired behaviour; feedback that rewards the wrong thing; no consequence for poor performance.

Resources and workload

Not enough time, people or equipment to work to the standard.

Selection and deployment

The wrong people in the role, or the right people deployed on the wrong work.

In most real cases the cause is a combination. A diagnosis that finds a genuine skill gap alongside a process problem and a workload problem is more useful than one that finds only the skill gap, because it explains why previous training did not stick.

A quick test

If the people concerned were offered a substantial reward to perform correctly tomorrow, could they? If yes, the gap is probably not knowledge or skill. Look at the environment, the process, the incentives and the resources first.

03Prelude’s diagnostic philosophy

Don’t start with the intervention. Start with the problem.

Prelude’s work rests on a small number of principles. They apply equally to a complex Defence programme and to a growing business.

  1. Diagnosis before intervention. No course, system, restructure or technology is recommended until the problem it is meant to solve has been defined and its cause understood.
  2. Evidence before assumption. Findings are labelled for what they are, so decision-makers can see how much weight each one will bear.
  3. Outcomes before outputs. A completed programme, framework or report is not evidence that performance improved.
  4. Proportionality. The depth of analysis matches the cost and risk of getting the decision wrong.
  5. The smallest effective combination. The aim is the least disruptive set of changes that will close the gap, not the most comprehensive programme.

Labelling findings

LabelMeaningExample
EvidenceDirectly observed or supported by data or documents.Error logs show 40 rejected submissions in March.
InferenceA reasonable conclusion drawn from evidence.Most rejections share the same missing field, so the form design is a likely cause.
AssumptionSomething believed but not yet tested.Staff have not been shown how to complete the form.
RecommendationWhat should happen next, and why.Redesign the field and test with five users before considering training.

The Prelude Performance & Capability Cycle

Every engagement, and the book Training Isn’t Always the Answer, follows the same five-stage cycle. The playbook’s chapters map onto it.

STAGE 1UnderstandWhat are we trying to achieve? Agree the outcome, the context and what good performance looks like.
STAGE 2DiagnoseWhy isn’t it happening now? Gather evidence and separate symptoms from causes.
STAGE 3DefineWhat needs to be different? Turn the diagnosis into clear requirements.
STAGE 4InterveneWhat is the smallest effective combination of changes?
STAGE 5ProveDid performance actually improve, against the agreed baseline?

The cycle is a method: it describes the order of work. The Prelude Capability Model™ in the next chapter is a model: it describes what capability is made of, and therefore where to look during the Diagnose stage.

04The Prelude Capability Model™

Performance is traced from mission down to evidence. When any layer is missing, capability fails, and training alone will not fix it.

1 · Mission & OutcomesWhat the organisation must achieve, and the standard it must achieve it to.
2 · Required CapabilityWhat the organisation must be able to do, reliably, to deliver those outcomes.
3 · BehavioursWhat people need to do differently, or consistently, in the work itself.
4 · Skills & KnowledgeWhat people need to know and be able to do to perform those behaviours.
5 · Governance & AssuranceHow the organisation sets standards, checks they are met and acts when they are not.
6 · Performance EvidenceThe observable proof that the outcome is being delivered.

Reading the model in two directions

Top-down: to design capability

Start with the mission and outcome. Define the capability required, then the behaviours, then the skills and knowledge. Decide how governance will confirm the standard, and what evidence will show it is being met. Training is designed last, against a defined requirement.

Bottom-up: to diagnose a problem

Start with the performance evidence that is missing or disappointing. Work upwards. Is assurance detecting the problem? Do people have the skills and knowledge? Are the behaviours defined? Is the required capability clear? Is the outcome itself agreed?

Diagnostic prompts for each layer

The prompts below are a practical aid for working through the model. They are not a scoring scheme.

LayerPrompts
Mission & OutcomesIs the outcome written down and agreed by the people who own it? Is the required standard explicit?
Required CapabilityCan we describe what the organisation must be able to do, in operational terms, without naming a solution?
BehavioursCould a manager recognise the desired behaviour if they saw it? Is it described in observable terms?
Skills & KnowledgeDo we have evidence that people lack the skill or knowledge, or are we assuming it?
Governance & AssuranceWho owns the standard? How would we know, today, if it were not being met?
Performance EvidenceWhat data or observation shows the current level of performance? Is there a baseline?

05Current versus required performance

A gap can only be measured between two points that have both been defined.

Many improvement efforts describe the current state in detail and the required state in a single aspiration (“better customer service”, “improved readiness”). Without a defined standard, there is no way to size the gap, choose an intervention or prove that anything changed.

Writing a performance statement

Describe required performance in three parts, borrowed from established objective-writing practice:

ElementQuestionExample
PerformanceWhat must be done, in observable terms?Complete and submit a safeguarding referral
ConditionsIn what circumstances, with what tools or information?Using the case management system, from a telephone disclosure
StandardHow well, how fast, how accurately?Within 24 hours, with all mandatory fields complete and no rejection by the receiving team

Describing current performance

Describe current performance in the same terms, with the evidence for it. If no evidence exists, that is a finding in itself: the organisation is not yet able to see the performance it is trying to improve.

Sizing the gap

  • How big is it? The difference between current and required performance, in the same units.
  • Who does it affect? All staff, one team, one shift, new starters only?
  • How long has it existed? A recent change often points to a recent cause.
  • What does it cost? In money, risk, time, safety, reputation or service quality.
  • What happens if nothing changes? The do-nothing position is the baseline every option is compared against.

06Finding the capability gap

Once the performance gap is defined, the next question is where in the capability system it originates.

Work across every factor before settling on any of them. The aim is to avoid the most common diagnostic error: stopping at the first plausible explanation, which is usually the one the original request already assumed.

Capability factors to examine
FactorSigns it may be contributing
Knowledge and skillExperienced and new staff perform differently; errors cluster on tasks people rarely perform; people cannot explain the correct method.
Process and standardsDifferent teams do the same task differently; procedures are out of date or contradict each other; nobody can show the written standard.
Systems, tools and informationWorkarounds are common; errors coincide with a system change; people spend time searching for information.
Roles and structureTasks fall between teams; people say “that’s not my job”; decisions wait for someone with authority.
Leadership and managementPerformance varies by manager more than by individual; priorities change often; little feedback is given.
Incentives and cultureThe desired behaviour is slower, harder or less rewarded than the current one; shortcuts are tolerated.
Resources and workloadPerformance drops at busy times; vacancies or absence are high; the standard is unachievable in the time allowed.
Governance and assuranceProblems are discovered late or by outsiders; nobody owns the standard; data is not reviewed.
External constraintsRegulation, suppliers, customers or partner organisations limit what can be changed.

Use Worksheet 3 to rate each factor and record the evidence for your view.

07Root-cause analysis

A symptom tells you where the problem appears. A cause tells you why it happens.

Three practical techniques

Five Whys

Ask “why?” repeatedly until the answer points to something the organisation can change. Five is a guide, not a rule. Stop when the answer is a cause that, if removed, would prevent the problem recurring.

Illustrative example
ProblemMonthly reports are submitted late.
Why?Team leaders finish them on the deadline day.
Why?The source data arrives two days before the deadline.
Why?The finance extract runs on a fixed date set four years ago.
CauseThe reporting timetable and the data timetable were never aligned. Report-writing training would not change this.

Fishbone (Ishikawa) analysis

Draw the problem as the “head” and use the capability factors in chapter 6 as the “bones”. Brainstorm possible causes under each, then test the most likely ones with evidence. The value is in forcing the group to look beyond the first explanation.

The Training vs Capability Decision Model™

A simple filter to apply once a gap has been identified:

QUESTIONIs the knowledge or skill genuinely missing?Test it: observe, ask people to demonstrate, compare performers.
IF YESA training or knowledge solution may helpConsider job aids, coaching and practice as well as formal training.
IF NOIt is a capability problemLook at structure, governance, leadership, process, systems and resources.

Pitfalls

  • Accepting the first cause offered, especially by the person who made the request.
  • Analysing in a meeting room without speaking to the people who do the work.
  • Treating every contributory factor as equally important.
  • Blaming individuals for what is a system problem.

08Gathering evidence

Gather enough evidence to make a defensible decision. Not more.

Sources of evidence

SourceUseful forWatch out for
Performance and operational dataSizing the gap; trends; baselinesData that measures activity rather than outcome
Observation of the workHow the task is really done; workaroundsPeople behaving differently when watched
Interviews and focus groupsCauses, barriers, contextHearing only from managers, or only from the loudest voices
Documents and proceduresThe intended standardThe difference between the written and the actual process
Incident, audit and complaint recordsWhere failures occur; riskUnder-reporting; records written to close a case
SurveysBreadth of views; perceptionLow response rates; leading questions
Comparison of high and typical performersWhat separates good from averageAssuming high performers’ methods are transferable without the same conditions

Triangulate

Look for at least two independent sources that point to the same conclusion before treating it as evidence. Where sources disagree, the disagreement is often the most useful finding.

Keep an evidence register

Record each finding, its source and its label (evidence, inference or assumption). This keeps the analysis honest and lets a sponsor see exactly what each recommendation rests on. Worksheet 4 provides a template.

Handle evidence with care

Interview notes, performance data and incident records may contain personal data. Agree in advance how evidence will be collected, stored, anonymised and retained, in line with your organisation’s data protection arrangements.

09The Capability Readiness Review™

The Capability Readiness Review is the first step before investing in training, consultancy or capability development. It tests how clearly an organisation can answer ten questions that determine whether an intervention is likely to work.

  1. What problem are you actually trying to solve?
  2. What behaviours need to change?
  3. What impact are you trying to achieve?
  4. What does success look like?
  5. How will success be measured?
  6. What evidence supports your current position?
  7. Have you tested whether training is the real issue?
  8. What organisational barriers exist?
  9. What risks exist if nothing changes?
  10. What capability is required to achieve the desired outcome?

In a facilitated review, the answers are tested against evidence to establish where the problem sits: in capability, leadership, process, governance, workforce or training, or in a combination of these.

10Common capability mistakes

Most failed improvement efforts fail for predictable reasons.

MistakeWhat it looks likeWhat to do instead
Prescribing before diagnosingA course, system or restructure is commissioned in the first meeting.Agree the outcome and the problem before discussing solutions.
Treating symptoms as causes“Staff make errors” is recorded as the cause.Ask why the errors happen and test each explanation.
Counting activity, not outcomesSuccess is reported as completions, attendance or satisfaction.Agree an outcome measure and a baseline before starting.
Governance as box-tickingProcess steps are completed but do not inform decisions.Use governance to ask and answer the questions that matter.
Buying a catalogueLearning content is bought against an undefined requirement.Define the capability required, then source against it.
Ignoring the environmentPeople are trained and returned to a workplace that prevents the behaviour.Address process, tools and management alongside any training.
One-size-fits-allEvery role receives the same intervention.Target the roles, tasks and teams where the gap actually sits.
No owner after go-liveImprovement fades once the project team moves on.Assign ownership of the standard and the measure before closure.

11Options appraisal and intervention selection

The aim is the smallest effective combination of changes, not the most comprehensive programme.

Build a long list

For each cause, list every credible way of addressing it, including options outside training:

People

Training, coaching, mentoring, practice, job aids, recruitment or selection changes, redeployment.

Process

Redesign, simplification, standard setting, checklists, removing steps.

Systems and information

Configuration changes, better information at the point of need, automation.

Organisation

Clarifying roles, decision rights, structure, workload balancing, management routines.

Always include do nothing and do the minimum. They set the baseline every other option must beat.

Appraise the short list

Agree the criteria with the decision-maker before scoring. Typical criteria:

  • Effectiveness: how directly does it address the cause?
  • Cost: to implement and to sustain.
  • Time: how quickly will it take effect?
  • Feasibility: does the organisation have the capacity and authority to implement it?
  • Risk: what could go wrong, and what are the dependencies?
  • Sustainability: will the improvement last once attention moves elsewhere?

Score options against the criteria, but treat the scores as a structured conversation rather than a calculation. Record the reasoning, because that is what the sponsor will need to defend the decision. Worksheet 5 provides a template.

12Prioritised improvement planning

A plan turns a recommendation into owned, sequenced and measurable actions.

Prioritise

Plot actions by impact on the outcome and effort to implement. Start with high-impact, low-effort actions to build momentum; plan high-impact, high-effort actions deliberately; question whether low-impact actions are needed at all.

Sequence

Some changes must come first. Training on a new process is wasted if the process is not yet stable. A capability framework is of limited use until roles are agreed. Record dependencies explicitly.

Assign ownership

Every action needs a named owner with the authority to deliver it, a target date and a measure of completion. Every outcome needs an owner who will still be accountable after the project ends.

Plan for sustainment

  • Who owns the standard once the change is in place?
  • How will new joiners be brought up to the standard?
  • What routine will check that the standard is still being met?
  • What would trigger a review?

Use Worksheet 6 to build the plan.

13Evaluation and measurement

Decide how success will be measured before the intervention starts, not after.

Agree a baseline

Without a baseline, any later improvement is a matter of opinion. Capture current performance, using the same measure you will use afterwards, over a period long enough to reflect normal variation.

Measure at more than one level

Kirkpatrick’s four levels remain a useful structure, provided the higher levels are planned rather than hoped for:

LevelQuestionExample measure
1 · ReactionDid participants find it relevant and usable?Short post-session feedback
2 · LearningDid knowledge or skill improve?Practical assessment against the standard
3 · BehaviourIs the new behaviour happening in the workplace?Observation, quality checks, manager review
4 · ResultsDid the outcome improve?The performance measure agreed at the start

For non-training interventions the same logic applies: did the change happen, is it being used, and did the outcome move?

Be careful with attribution

Outcomes are influenced by many factors. Where you can, compare with a group that has not yet received the intervention, track the measure over time, and record other changes that happened in the same period. Report what the evidence supports and no more.

Leading and lagging measures

Lagging measures (outcomes) tell you whether you succeeded, but only after the event. Leading measures (behaviours, process compliance, early quality checks) tell you whether you are on track. A good evaluation plan includes both.
