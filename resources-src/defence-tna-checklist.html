BEFORE YOU STARTAbout this checklist

A Training Needs Analysis is a decision-support process. Done well, it tells the organisation what performance is required, where the gap is, whether training will close it, and which option offers the best value. Done as a paperwork exercise, it delays decisions without improving them.

Purpose

Use this checklist to plan a TNA, to check progress during one, or to review a completed TNA before it is endorsed. It is written for individual training TNAs conducted under the Defence Systems Approach to Training (DSAT). Much of it applies to collective training and to non-Defence organisations, but JSP 822 sets out separate direction for collective training.

How to use it

  1. Work through each of the thirteen sections. Tick Done, Partly, Not done or N/A for each check.
  2. Record where the evidence can be found: a document reference, a register entry or a meeting record.
  3. Treat every “Partly” or “Not done” as a question to answer, not a box to fill. Some will be acceptable risks in a proportionate TNA; record why.
  4. Summarise the position on the summary page and record actions in the action log.
Policy requirement or Prelude guidance?

Each check carries a basis tag. JSP 822 marks a check that relates to a role, stage or product named in JSP 822 Defence Direction and Guidance for Training and Education (V7.0), Volume 2: Defence Individual Training. Prelude marks Prelude’s practitioner guidance: good practice, not policy.

The tag shows where a check connects to policy. It does not reproduce or replace the policy text. JSP 822 distinguishes between what must be done (mandatory), what should be done (recommended; if it is not followed, the Senior Responsible Owner owns the resulting risk) and what could be done (good practice). Always confirm the exact requirement in the current edition and in any single-Service policy that sits beneath it.

CONTEXTWhere TNA sits in DSAT

DSAT is organised into four elements, supported by governance through the Management of Training System (MTS):

1  AnalysisTraining Needs Analysis: is training needed, and if so what and how?
2  DesignDesigning the training to meet the agreed requirement.
3  DeliveryConducting the training.
4  AssuranceEvaluation and assurance that training is effective and remains so.

The three stages of a TNA

JSP 822 Volume 2 describes the TNA in three stages:

Stage 1  Scoping
  • Scoping Exercise
  • Risk Register and Assumptions Register
  • Scoping Exercise Report
Stage 2  Analysis
  • Role Analysis and Role Performance Statement and/or framework
  • Training Gap Analysis
  • Initial Training Objectives
  • Training Options Analysis
  • Training Needs Report
Stage 3  Evaluation
  • Training Needs Evaluation, carried out within the Assurance element

Key roles

RoleSummary
Training Requirements Authority (TRA)Represents the end-user of the trained output. Owns the performance requirement (the Role Performance Statement and/or framework) and evaluates training effect.
Training Delivery Authority (TDA)Responsible for training delivery, though not always for conducting the training itself. Leads design. Must be distinct from the TRA.
Training ProviderThe school, college, unit or organisation that delivers the training.
TNA Steering GroupProvides governance and direction for the TNA where one is established.

Summaries are Prelude’s paraphrase for orientation only. Refer to JSP 822 for the authoritative definitions and responsibilities.

Proportionality

Not every requirement justifies a full TNA. A minor change to an existing course may need only a short scoping exercise and a documented decision; a new platform or a safety-critical role may need the full analysis. Whatever the depth, the decision about depth should be explicit, recorded and owned.

THE CHECKLISTSections 1 to 13

1Requirement definition and set-up

Establish why the TNA is needed, who owns it and how far it should go.

#CheckBasisDonePartlyNot doneN/AEvidence / reference
1.1The trigger for the TNA is recorded (new or changed equipment, role, policy, threat, structure or an identified performance shortfall).Prelude
1.2The Training Requirements Authority (TRA), representing the end-user of the trained output, is identified.JSP 822
1.3The Training Delivery Authority (TDA) is identified, and is distinct from the TRA.JSP 822
1.4A sponsor or Senior Responsible Owner who will own residual risk is named.Prelude
1.5Governance for the TNA (for example a TNA Steering Group) and its terms of reference are agreed.JSP 822
1.6A Scoping Exercise has been carried out to bound the analysis.JSP 822
1.7Scope, exclusions, constraints, timescales and resources for the TNA are recorded in a Scoping Exercise Report.JSP 822
1.8The depth of analysis is proportionate to the cost, risk and complexity of the requirement.Prelude

2Stakeholder engagement

Make sure the people who will use, deliver, fund and assure the training are involved early.

#CheckBasisDonePartlyNot doneN/AEvidence / reference
2.1Stakeholders are identified and mapped: TRA, TDA, training provider, end-user units, subject matter experts, equipment or project teams, finance and assurance.Prelude
2.2Roles and responsibilities for the TNA are agreed (a RACI or equivalent).Prelude
2.3Subject matter experts with current operational experience are available for role and task analysis.Prelude
2.4The views of current role holders and their line managers are captured, not only those of headquarters staff.Prelude
2.5There is an agreed route for resolving disagreements about requirements.Prelude

3Operational context

Understand the environment in which the role will be performed.

#CheckBasisDonePartlyNot doneN/AEvidence / reference
3.1The operational or business outcome the role contributes to is stated.Prelude
3.2The environment, equipment, conditions and constraints in which the role is performed are described.Prelude
3.3Relevant doctrine, policy, safety and legislative requirements are identified.Prelude
3.4Planned changes (equipment, structures, concepts of use, manning) that will affect the role are identified, with dates.Prelude
3.5Interfaces with other roles, teams, Services or partners are understood.Prelude

4Performance requirements

Define what competent performance looks like before deciding how to achieve it.

#CheckBasisDonePartlyNot doneN/AEvidence / reference
4.1Role Analysis has been carried out to establish what the role holder must do.JSP 822
4.2A Role Performance Statement (and/or framework) records the tasks, conditions and standards for the role.JSP 822
4.3Standards are observable and measurable rather than descriptive.Prelude
4.4The Role Performance Statement is endorsed by the TRA.JSP 822
4.5Performance requirements are traceable to the operational outcome identified in section 3.Prelude

5Role and task analysis

Break the role down to the level needed to make sound training decisions.

#CheckBasisDonePartlyNot doneN/AEvidence / reference
5.1Tasks are identified and broken down into sub-tasks where needed.Prelude
5.2Each task is assessed for difficulty, importance and frequency (DIF) or an equivalent prioritisation method.Prelude
5.3Safety-critical, high-risk and infrequently performed tasks are flagged.Prelude
5.4Tasks are validated with current role holders and subject matter experts.Prelude
5.5The analysis records which tasks need to be performed to standard on completion of training and which can be developed in the workplace.Prelude

6Knowledge, skills and attitudes

Identify what the role holder must know, be able to do and how they must behave.

#CheckBasisDonePartlyNot doneN/AEvidence / reference
6.1Knowledge, skills and attitudes (or behaviours) are identified for each task or task group.Prelude
6.2Underpinning knowledge is distinguished from knowledge that can be supported by job aids or reference material.Prelude
6.3Any qualifications, accreditations or licences required for the role are identified.Prelude
6.4Behavioural requirements (for example judgement, teamwork, leadership) are described in observable terms.Prelude

7Existing capability and gap analysis

Compare required performance with what the target audience can already do.

#CheckBasisDonePartlyNot doneN/AEvidence / reference
7.1The target audience is described: entry standard, experience, numbers and throughput.Prelude
7.2Existing training, qualifications and workplace experience are mapped against the requirement.Prelude
7.3A Training Gap Analysis identifies the difference between the required performance and the target audience’s entry standard.JSP 822
7.4The gap is supported by evidence (assessment data, observation, incident or performance data), not only by opinion.Prelude
7.5Initial Training Objectives are derived from the identified gap.JSP 822

8Training and non-training causes

Confirm that training is the right response to each part of the gap.

#CheckBasisDonePartlyNot doneN/AEvidence / reference
8.1For each significant gap, the analysis tests whether the knowledge or skill is genuinely missing.Prelude
8.2Non-training causes are considered: equipment, process, procedures, manning, workload, structure, leadership, information and incentives.Prelude
8.3Non-training findings are recorded and passed to the appropriate owner, rather than discarded.Prelude
8.4The analysis states which parts of the gap training is expected to close, and which it is not.Prelude

9Options analysis

Identify and compare the ways the training requirement could be met.

#CheckBasisDonePartlyNot doneN/AEvidence / reference
9.1A Training Options Analysis considers a realistic range of delivery options.JSP 822
9.2Options include different methods and media: formal courses, workplace training, coaching, technology-enhanced learning, simulation and job aids.Prelude
9.3Options consider where and by whom training is delivered, and the balance between training establishment and workplace.Prelude
9.4Each option is assessed for effectiveness, cost (including whole-life cost), time, risk, capacity and supportability.Prelude
9.5The do-minimum option is assessed alongside more ambitious options.Prelude
9.6The recommended option, and the reasoning for it, is recorded.Prelude

10Evidence and assumptions

Make the basis of the analysis visible.

#CheckBasisDonePartlyNot doneN/AEvidence / reference
10.1A Risk Register and an Assumptions Register are maintained throughout the TNA.JSP 822
10.2Sources of evidence are recorded and can be produced if challenged.Prelude
10.3Findings are labelled as evidence, inference or assumption.Prelude
10.4Assumptions with a significant effect on the recommendation have an owner and a date for validation.Prelude

11Governance and assurance

Make sure the analysis is approved, recorded and evaluated.

#CheckBasisDonePartlyNot doneN/AEvidence / reference
11.1Decision points and approvals are recorded, including who approved what and when.Prelude
11.2The Training Needs Report is submitted to and endorsed by the appropriate authority.JSP 822
11.3Configuration control is in place for TNA products so later changes are traceable.Prelude
11.4A Training Needs Evaluation is planned to confirm, after implementation, whether the TNA met its purpose.JSP 822
11.5Links to internal and external validation of the resulting training are identified.JSP 822

12Risks and dependencies

Understand what could prevent the recommendation from working.

#CheckBasisDonePartlyNot doneN/AEvidence / reference
12.1Risks to delivery (funding, instructors, equipment availability, estate, timescales) are identified and owned.Prelude
12.2Dependencies on equipment programmes, other TNAs, contracts or policy decisions are recorded.Prelude
12.3The residual training gap, and the risk of accepting it, is stated and owned.Prelude
12.4Safety and care and welfare considerations for the proposed training are identified.Prelude

13Recommendations and reporting

Present a clear, defensible recommendation.

#CheckBasisDonePartlyNot doneN/AEvidence / reference
13.1A Training Needs Report sets out the analysis, options and recommendation.JSP 822
13.2Recommendations are specific, costed where possible, prioritised and assigned to owners.Prelude
13.3Non-training recommendations are presented alongside training recommendations.Prelude
13.4The report states how success will be measured and when it will be reviewed.Prelude
13.5The report can be understood by a decision-maker who did not take part in the analysis.Prelude

Common pitfalls

  • Starting with the course. The TNA is commissioned to justify a solution already chosen.
  • Desk-based task analysis. Tasks are listed from documents without validation by current role holders.
  • Discarding non-training findings. Equipment, process or manning issues are noted and then lost.
  • One option. The options analysis compares the preferred option with nothing.
  • No evaluation plan. Nobody can later say whether the TNA’s recommendation worked.
