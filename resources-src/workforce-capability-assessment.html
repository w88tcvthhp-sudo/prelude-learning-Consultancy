BEFORE YOU STARTAbout this toolkit

Most organisations know roughly where their people are strong and weak. Fewer can show which gaps put delivery at risk, which can wait, and which will not be fixed by training at all.

This toolkit takes you through eight steps, from the outcomes the workforce must deliver to a prioritised, owned improvement plan. It works for a single team or a whole organisation, and for commercial businesses, SMEs, public bodies, charities and regulated industries alike. Scale the effort to the decision: a small business might complete it in a morning for five roles; a large organisation might use it to structure a programme of work.

Capability and competency

In this toolkit, a capability is something the organisation needs people to be able to do (for example “manage a complaint to resolution” or “plan and run a safe site inspection”). Competency describes an individual’s demonstrated ability against that capability at the level the role requires. Organisational capability also depends on processes, systems, structure and leadership, which is why step 7 looks beyond training.

Use what you already have

If your organisation or sector already uses a competency or skills framework, use its definitions and levels rather than creating new ones. This toolkit provides a structure for the assessment, not a replacement framework. Results are indicative and depend on the quality of the evidence gathered; they are not a validated psychometric or audit measure.

STEPS 1 – 2Workforce requirements, roles and responsibilities

Step 1 · Define what the workforce must deliver

Start with outcomes, not with people. Capture what the organisation must achieve over the period you are planning for and what will drive demand for capability.

Outcomes the workforce must deliver (services, products, standards, targets)
Changes on the horizon (growth, new services, technology, regulation, restructuring)
Standards that are non-negotiable (safety, legal, regulatory, contractual, quality)
Planning horizon and scope of this assessment

Step 2 · Identify the roles in scope

List the roles (not the individuals) that deliver those outcomes. Rate each role’s criticality: the consequence for the organisation if the role could not be performed to standard.

CriticalityMeaning
HighFailure would stop a core service, create a safety, legal or regulatory breach, or cause serious financial or reputational harm.
MediumFailure would significantly reduce quality, efficiency or customer experience, but could be contained for a period.
LowFailure would cause inconvenience but could be covered or deferred.

STEPS 3 – 4Capabilities, standards and mapping

Step 3 · Define the capabilities that matter

Identify the capabilities needed to deliver the outcomes from step 1. Keep the list short enough to assess properly: for most teams, eight to fifteen capabilities is enough. Include technical capabilities, and the behavioural and management capabilities that make them work.

  • Describe each capability in observable terms: what someone does, not what they know about.
  • Separate capabilities that everyone needs from those specific to certain roles.
  • Note any capability that requires a qualification, licence or authorisation.

Set the required standard

Use a proficiency scale to describe the level each role needs. If you have no existing scale, the example below is a practical starting point:

LevelLabelDescription
0Not requiredThe role does not need this capability.
1AwarenessUnderstands what it is and when it applies; knows who to go to.
2WorkingPerforms routine tasks to standard with occasional guidance.
3PractitionerPerforms independently to standard, including non-routine situations.
4ExpertSets standards, solves complex problems, develops and assures others.

Example scale only. Adapt the labels and descriptions to your context or replace with your existing framework.

Step 4 · Map capabilities to roles

Use Template A to record the required level of each capability for each role. This is the “required” picture against which the current state will be compared. Validate it with the people who manage and perform the roles: requirements written only by HR or a central team are often over- or under-specified.

Check the requirement before assessing people

If managers disagree about the level a role needs, resolve that first. A gap measured against a disputed standard will not be acted on.

Template A · Role capability matrix (required levels)

Enter capabilities across the top and roles down the side. In each cell, record the required level (0–4). Shade or mark cells where the capability is critical to the role.

STEP 5Assess the current state and gather evidence

The quality of the assessment depends almost entirely on the quality of the evidence.

Assessment methods

MethodStrengthsLimitations
Self-assessmentQuick, scalable, engages staffPeople over- or under-rate themselves; best combined with another method
Manager assessmentInformed by day-to-day observationVaries by manager; affected by relationships and recent events
Observation or work samplingShows real performance in contextTime-consuming; behaviour may change when observed
Practical assessment or testConsistent and defensibleCostly to design; may not reflect real conditions
Records and dataObjective: qualifications, quality data, errors, complaintsShows outcomes, not always the cause

Good practice

  • Use at least two methods for high-criticality capabilities.
  • Calibrate: have managers compare and discuss their ratings before finalising, so the same level means the same thing across teams.
  • Assess against the role standard, not against colleagues.
  • Record the evidence for each rating in the evidence register (Template D).
  • Explain to staff why the assessment is happening and how results will be used. Assessments perceived as performance management produce defensive answers.
Data protection

Individual capability ratings are personal data. Agree the purpose, who can see individual results, how long they are kept and how they will be used before you start, in line with your organisation’s data protection arrangements. Reporting at team or role level is often sufficient for planning.

Ask “why?” before “what training?”

For each Priority 1 and 2 gap, test the cause. Is the knowledge or skill genuinely missing, or are capable people prevented from performing by process, tools, workload, unclear roles or management? The answer determines the response in step 7.

STEP 7Development priorities and non-training interventions

Match the response to the cause. Training is one option among several.

STEP 8 · TEMPLATE EPrioritised improvement plan and evaluation

Turn priority gaps and risks into owned actions with a measure of success. Start with Priority 1. Record dependencies: training on a process that is about to change is wasted effort.

Gap / risk ref.ActionType (training / non-training)Depends onOwnerDueMeasure of success

Evaluation

Decide now how you will know the plan has worked, and when you will check.

Baseline: current levels from Template B, and any operational measures
Leading indicators: early signs the actions are working (for example completions, observed practice, quality checks)
Outcome measures: the operational results the capability should improve
Reassessment date and who will carry it out

Repeat the assessment for priority roles at a planned interval, typically six to twelve months, using the same scale and methods so that results are comparable.
